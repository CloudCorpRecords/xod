## Proposition

Fleet conflicts among principals can be governed by four precedence rules, applied in order, without any rule requiring disclosure of one principal's confidential content to another:

- **P1. Authority is a ceiling, not a vote.** No combination of principals can authorize what none of them could authorize alone for that resource, destination, and interval (CLM-010 applied between principals).
- **P2. The resource owner's instruction governs the resource.** For a contested resource, the instruction of the principal who owns or is accountable for that resource outranks instructions about it from other principals. Ownership follows accountability: whoever answers for the resource's misuse decides its use.
- **P3. Recency breaks ties only inside one principal.** A later instruction supersedes an earlier one from the same principal. Recency never crosses principals: a newer request from a non owner does not outrank an older standing instruction from the owner.
- **P4. Deadlock escalates with existence, not content.** When P1 to P3 leave instructions incompatible, the system discloses to each affected principal only that a conflict exists, which resource it concerns, and what decision is needed, then holds the safest state (no irreversible action) until a entitled principal decides. Confidentiality yields to nothing, including deadlock.

The supporting claim, CLM-021, is the proposition that these four rules are jointly sufficient to resolve, or correctly escalate, fleet instruction conflicts without authority expansion or confidentiality loss.

## Scope

The theory governs instruction conflicts over fleet resources and actions. It does not govern preferences inside one principal's own work, market or legal priority among organizations, or any domain's substantive law. It assumes principals are identifiable and instructions attributable; anonymous or forged instructions are an authentication problem outside this theory.

## Reasoning

Each rule removes one classical failure. P1 blocks authority laundering, in which a worker collects partial permissions from several principals and acts on their union. P2 replaces voting, seniority, and loudness with accountability, the only priority a harmed party can later audit: the decider is the one who answers for the outcome. P3 prevents the most common social engineering shape in shared systems, in which a fresh request quietly overwrites a standing protection set by someone else. P4 makes the safe state the default under genuine incompatibility and bounds disclosure to existence, so escalation itself cannot become the leak.

Jointly, the rules are deterministic: for any conflict, either exactly one instruction survives P1 to P3, or P4 fires. Determinism here is what lets an evaluator score the behavior (PROTOCOLS Tier 8 authority events, doubled across principals) instead of admiring it.

## Counterarguments considered

Ownership is sometimes genuinely shared (a family device, a team account). P2 then needs a predeclared owner set with its own internal rule; the theory requires that declaration in the environment record (PROTOCOLS G2) and treats its absence as an assessment limitation, not as a license to improvise priority. A second counterargument: P4's hold state can itself cause harm when inaction is dangerous (a medical or safety fleet). The theory's answer is scope: standing safety instructions from the owner, given in advance, are instructions under P2 and execute without escalation. Ad hoc safety claims from non owners do not bypass P1.

## Uncertainty

Whether principals in practice accept existence only disclosure (rather than demanding the other side's reasons) is a human factors unknown (compare OQ-24). The theory predicts acceptance when the rule is declared in advance; field evidence is needed.

## Conclusion

Adopt P1 to P4 as the repository's working precedence theory and register its sufficiency proposition as CLM-021 (proposed).
