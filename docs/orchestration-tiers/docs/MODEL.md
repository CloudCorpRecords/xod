# The Ladder as a Formal Model

## 1. Purpose of the model

The theory explains distinctions. The model turns those distinctions into repeatable assessment practice. It defines what a tier claim means, which gates are mandatory, what evidence can support a verdict, how confidence is expressed, and how claims expire or regress.

The model is intentionally stricter than ordinary product language. Its purpose is not to make systems look weak. Its purpose is to prevent weak evidence from being described with strong words.

## 2. Unit of assessment

The unit is never a brand, model name, or organization in the abstract. The unit is a configuration in an environment over a stated interval.

A complete assessment subject includes:

- system name;
- version, build, model, and material configuration when known;
- tools and integrations enabled;
- memory and state mechanisms in use;
- authority envelope;
- human oversight arrangement;
- environment and device classes;
- task protocol and rubric version;
- dates of observation;
- evidence packet location.

If any element changes materially, the old claim does not automatically transfer. It may remain historical evidence, but the new configuration requires its own assessment or an explicit equivalence argument.

## 3. Tier claim structure

A tier claim has the following logical form:

> For system S, configuration C, environment E, and interval T, the evidence supports verdict V at Tier N under rubric R, subject to limitations L.

The preferred claim syntax is:

```text
CLAIM
system: <name>
version_or_configuration: <identifier or UNKNOWN>
environment: <devices, tools, accounts, and relevant constraints>
interval: <start date> to <end date>
tier: <0 to 8>
verdict: <PROVEN, SUPPORTED, MIXED, UNSUPPORTED, REFUTED, or UNKNOWN>
confidence: <LOW, MODERATE, or HIGH>
evidence: <links to evidence packet and run records>
limitations: <material unknowns and exclusions>
rubric: <repository version or commit>
```

A claim without an environment and interval is incomplete. A claim that omits limitations is suspect, especially at Tiers 6 through 8.

## 4. Mandatory gates

Each tier file lists mandatory criteria. A mandatory gate is not a preference or a scoring component that can be offset by excellence elsewhere. If a mandatory gate is absent, the tier verdict cannot be PROVEN.

Gates are cumulative in intent:

- Tier 1 presumes Tier 0 accuracy and restraint.
- Tier 2 presumes grounded identification of the target and expected effect.
- Tier 3 presumes verified action where the sequence has side effects.
- Tier 4 presumes source discipline from Tier 1 and honest treatment of failed reads.
- Tier 5 presumes workers can perform their assigned lower tier work and the parent can verify decisive results.
- Tier 6 presumes durable state, verified actions, and honest status reporting.
- Tier 7 presumes long horizon state and disciplined recurring execution.
- Tier 8 presumes claims, authority, verification, and delivery accounting across every participating unit.

A protocol may observe an upper tier behavior before all lower tiers have been separately tested. The assessment should then mark untested lower gates as UNKNOWN rather than infer them from the impressive result. In a full "reliable through Tier N" claim, all mandatory gates at and below N must be satisfied.

## 5. Evidence classes

Evidence is classified by proximity to the event and independence from the claimant.

### Class A: Independent direct evidence

Artifacts or observations produced outside the assessed system's self report: destination records, independently captured logs, external timestamps, third party confirmations, reproducible test harness output, or direct evaluator observation with raw artifacts retained.

Class A evidence has the greatest weight, especially for side effects and delivery.

### Class B: System generated primary artifacts

Logs, state files, run records, transcripts, hashes, plans, and outputs generated by the assessed system or its infrastructure. Class B evidence is valuable when complete, timestamped, internally consistent, and open to inspection. It is weaker when the system can edit or selectively export the record without detection.

### Class C: Structured self report

A report by the system or its operator that identifies tasks, outcomes, evidence locations, failures, and unknowns in a checkable structure. Class C evidence can support a preliminary verdict but is normally insufficient alone for PROVEN at higher tiers.

### Class D: Narrative or promotional assertion

A claim in prose, a selected demonstration, a testimonial, or an edited presentation without underlying run artifacts. Class D material may motivate investigation. It does not establish a tier.

### Class E: Missing, contradictory, or invalid evidence

Failed reads, absent logs, inconsistent timestamps, duplicated outcomes, results that cannot be tied to a run, or artifacts contradicted by a destination. Class E evidence does not always refute capability, but it blocks strong verdicts until resolved and may support REFUTED for a specific claim about a specific run.

## 6. Verdict taxonomy

### PROVEN

All mandatory gates were observed under the stated protocol, replication requirements were met, negative outcomes were included, and no material contradiction remains unresolved. PROVEN is always bounded by system, configuration, environment, interval, and rubric. It does not mean universally or permanently true.

### SUPPORTED

Substantial evidence points toward the tier, but replication, independence, coverage, or one noncentral evidentiary requirement is incomplete. SUPPORTED is a meaningful positive verdict, not a euphemism for failure.

### MIXED

Evidence shows both material satisfaction and material failure of gates, or performance varies sharply by condition. MIXED is appropriate when averaging the result into a single positive or negative label would mislead.

### UNSUPPORTED

The claim has been examined and the available evidence is insufficient to carry it, without a decisive contradiction. UNSUPPORTED differs from UNKNOWN because an assessment effort occurred and the packet failed to establish the proposition.

### REFUTED

Evidence contradicts a mandatory element of the claim. Examples include a claimed delivery absent at the destination, a recurring job shown not to run, a supposed parallel process shown to be serial, or a claimed authority boundary repeatedly violated. REFUTED applies to the claim as stated, not necessarily to every possible capability of the system.

### UNKNOWN

No adequate assessment basis exists. The evidence may be inaccessible, the protocol may not have run, the environment may be unspecified, or the available observations may not address the tier. UNKNOWN is the correct verdict when honesty requires declining to judge.

## 7. Confidence

Confidence describes the assessment, not the personality of the assessor.

- **LOW:** one or few observations, limited independence, substantial missing context, or narrow conditions.
- **MODERATE:** repeated observations with inspectable artifacts and manageable unknowns.
- **HIGH:** preregistered or otherwise disciplined protocol, repeated trials, independent destination evidence, negative results included, and major alternative explanations addressed.

A verdict and confidence are separate. A claim can be REFUTED with HIGH confidence, SUPPORTED with LOW confidence, or UNKNOWN with HIGH confidence that the evidence is insufficient.

## 8. Replication requirements

Replication should scale with tier and consequence.

### Minimum orientation

- Tiers 0 to 2: repeated tasks across superficial variations, with exact read backs for actions.
- Tiers 3 to 4: multiple sequences and research questions, including an induced or naturally occurring failure and conflicting sources.
- Tier 5: multiple fan out tasks with worker failure, duplication checks, and independent verification of decisive outputs.
- Tier 6: at least one interruption, one steering event, one restart or equivalent state threat, and a status inquiry based on observed state.
- Tier 7: multiple scheduled cycles across the natural period of the job, including a no change cycle, a change cycle, and a failure or disabled state handled according to the protocol.
- Tier 8: multiple coordinated operations involving more than one device or agent class, a contention event, an authority boundary test, destination verification at each endpoint, and an audit reconstruction.

These are model defaults, not universal numerical law. A deviation is permissible when recorded and defended. It is not permissible to silently use a weaker protocol while retaining the language of the stronger one.

## 9. Scoring and partial credit

The model permits diagnostic subscores inside an assessment, but the tier verdict is gated rather than averaged. A system may receive credit for observed subcapabilities even when the tier verdict is MIXED or UNSUPPORTED.

For example, an assessment might state:

- parallel dispatch observed;
- worker isolation partially observed;
- parent verification absent;
- duplicate work observed;
- Tier 5 verdict MIXED.

This is more informative than assigning 72 percent of Tier 5. Percentages may describe task completion within a run, but they do not replace gate logic.

## 10. Claim expiration

A claim expires when any of the following occurs:

- the stated interval ends and the claim is phrased as current rather than historical;
- a material model, tool, memory, scheduler, permission, or interface change occurs;
- the environment changes in a way that affects mandatory gates;
- contradictory evidence appears;
- the rubric changes materially;
- the evidence packet becomes inaccessible or cannot be authenticated.

Expiration does not mean the historical claim was false. It means the claim should no longer be used as a present tense description without renewal.

## 11. Regression

A regression is a later observation showing that a previously supported mandatory behavior now fails under comparable conditions. Regressions should be recorded with the earlier claim, the new evidence, the suspected change if known, and the revised verdict.

A regression at a lower tier suspends confidence in dependent higher tier claims until the relationship is examined. The higher tier need not be automatically REFUTED. It becomes at risk, and its verdict should be revised according to the new evidence.

## 12. Amendments to the model

The ladder can be amended. An amendment proposal should identify:

- the ambiguity or counterexample motivating the change;
- whether the issue concerns definition, gates, evidence, thresholds, or vocabulary;
- effects on prior claims;
- whether prior assessments require reinterpretation;
- dissenting views.

Amendments are deliberative acts, not silent edits. When a rubric changes, new claims should name the rubric version so older verdicts remain interpretable.
