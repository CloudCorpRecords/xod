# Theory of Agent Orchestration Capability

## 1. The object of study

An agent system is not only a model. In any realistic deployment it is a changing assembly of model, prompts, tools, memory, state stores, permissions, schedulers, user interfaces, external services, human approvals, and recovery mechanisms. Orchestration is the discipline by which that assembly turns an intention into coordinated activity and accounts for the result.

The ladder studies orchestration capability. It does not measure intelligence in the abstract, eloquence, benchmark knowledge, or the impressiveness of a final artifact in isolation. It asks a narrower and more operational question: under stated conditions, what kinds of work can the whole system initiate, coordinate, sustain, verify, and answer for?

This distinction matters because the same model can appear radically more capable when surrounded by durable state, scoped tools, independent workers, reliable clocks, and a disciplined verification loop. Conversely, a strong model can be made operationally weak by a serial interface, missing state, unclear ownership, or tools that report success without observable effects.

## 2. Four axes that must not be collapsed

### 2.1 Capability

Capability is what a system can do at least under some favorable conditions. It concerns reach: can it retrieve, write, reason, delegate, schedule, coordinate, or recover at all?

Capability claims are vulnerable to selection effects. A curated demonstration often searches a large space of attempts and displays the successful branch. It proves possibility in that instance, subject to the authenticity of the record, but says little about the probability of success on the next attempt.

### 2.2 Reliability

Reliability is capability made repeatable under variation. It includes success rate, variance, failure detection, recovery, and the stability of results across time, task phrasing, load, interruptions, and modest environmental change.

A system may be capable of Tier 6 work and reliable only through Tier 3. The ladder's phrase "reliable through Tier N" is intended to keep this distinction visible. Reliability is also asymmetric: an occasional brilliant success does not cancel a recurring failure to perform a mandatory lower level function.

### 2.3 Authority

Authority is what the system is permitted to do, for whom, in which scope, with what approval, and with what ability to delegate that permission. Authority is not generated by capability. A system that can send a message has not thereby acquired authority to send every message. A worker that receives a broad instruction from an orchestrator does not automatically inherit every permission the orchestrator's user granted for a different purpose.

Authority must be measured as part of an orchestration claim because upper tier work multiplies handoffs. Every handoff is a possible point of authority expansion, confusion, or leakage. A fleet system that coordinates efficiently while silently widening permissions is not advanced in the relevant sense. It is uncontrolled.

### 2.4 Autonomy

Autonomy is the degree to which a system can select and execute steps without contemporaneous human direction. Autonomy is not the same as independence from oversight, nor is more autonomy always better. A system can be highly autonomous inside a narrow envelope and appropriately supervised outside it.

Autonomy should be described by scope, duration, reversibility, and escalation conditions. "It worked alone" is incomplete. A serious claim states what decisions it made alone, which actions were preauthorized, what would have caused it to stop, and how a human could inspect or intervene.

## 3. Why a single demonstration proves almost nothing

A single successful run establishes, at most, that one trajectory existed through a state space at one time. It does not establish any of the following:

- that the system can find that trajectory again;
- that the run was not selected from failures;
- that the environment was representative;
- that hidden human assistance did not supply the missing step;
- that side effects were complete and nonduplicative;
- that the result reached its intended destination;
- that the system knew it had succeeded for the right reason;
- that the same method will survive delay, restart, load, or source conflict.

This is not cynicism. It is the ordinary logic of evidence. The evidential weight of a run rises with preregistered tasks, transparent denominators, independent observation, repeated trials, varied conditions, negative result reporting, and destination verification.

## 4. Composition and bottlenecks

Agent systems are compositional. Their final behavior emerges from layers that can succeed or fail semi independently:

1. interpretation of intent;
2. retrieval of context;
3. planning;
4. tool selection;
5. execution;
6. state tracking;
7. worker coordination;
8. verification;
9. reporting and delivery;
10. recovery.

The ladder follows a bottleneck principle: a mandatory layer failure caps the meaningful tier claim even if other layers perform spectacularly. A brilliant planner connected to an unverified action layer is not a reliable actor. A fleet coordinator without resource claims can create damage faster, not demonstrate higher orchestration. A recurring job without delivery accounting can generate results that never reach anyone.

Composition also creates emergent failures. Two individually sound agents can duplicate work, overwrite one another, amplify an erroneous premise, or each assume the other verified a result. Tier 5 and Tier 8 therefore measure coordination properties that do not exist in a single agent test.

## 5. Task states, product states, and deployment states

Confusion often arises because the word "agent" is used for several different arrangements. This repository separates three analytical dimensions.

### 5.1 Task state

A task state describes the work itself: its duration, dependency structure, parallelism, recurrence, side effects, number of destinations, and number of principals. A one line factual answer and a week long monitoring obligation differ as tasks even if the same system performs both.

### 5.2 Product state

A product state describes the packaged system offered to a user: interface, defaults, data handling, support model, update mechanism, and declared limits. A research prototype and a supported product may share a model while differing sharply in accountability and operational discipline.

### 5.3 Deployment state

A deployment state describes how the system is actually instantiated in an environment. Useful analytical categories include:

- **Model only:** no external action or live retrieval beyond the conversation.
- **Tool augmented:** the model can call tools, but coordination remains mostly conversational and transient.
- **Workflow bound:** predefined sequences and state transitions constrain execution.
- **Orchestrated:** a coordinating layer plans, delegates, tracks, interrupts, and verifies work.
- **Persistent:** work and memory survive sessions through explicit state, schedules, and recovery procedures.
- **Fleet:** multiple devices, agents, identities, or principals are coordinated under claims and authority boundaries.

These are analytical categories, not maturity commandments. A workflow bound system may be preferable to an orchestrated one when the task is narrow and the cost of error is high. A fleet deployment creates new capability and new failure modes at the same time.

## 6. Determinism of verdicts

The model aspires to verdict determinism: the same evidence packet, evaluated under the same rubric version, should yield the same verdict. This does not mean the underlying agent behaves deterministically. It means assessment should not depend on which evaluator happened to read the packet or how persuasive the system sounded.

Disagreement remains possible and useful. Under this theory, a disagreement must be traceable to at least one explicit source:

- the evaluators classify an artifact differently;
- one evaluator has evidence the other lacks;
- a mandatory criterion is ambiguous;
- the environment differs in a material way;
- a threshold or replication rule is disputed;
- a run is alleged to be invalid, contaminated, or unrepresentative.

If none of these can be identified, the disagreement is probably rhetorical rather than evidential. The deliberation protocol requires participants to locate the source before repeating their conclusions more loudly.

## 7. Regression and ladder order

Capability is not a monotonic score that only moves upward. A change can improve synthesis while damaging grounding. A new scheduler can create duplicate notifications. A faster worker pool can increase contention. For that reason, regression is reported by tier and lower tier regressions take priority over upper tier novelty.

Ladder order encodes a dependency claim: higher orchestration is not trustworthy if its mandatory foundations are unstable. A Tier 8 result built on Tier 2 guesses may be real as an event, but it is fragile as a capability claim. The model therefore distinguishes event success from reliable tier attainment.

## 8. Human oversight as part of the measured system

Human oversight is often treated as an external correction to an allegedly autonomous system. That framing hides the actual unit of performance. If a human quietly supplies missing context, chooses among hidden alternatives, repairs a tool failure, or verifies every output, the deployed system includes that labor.

An assessment should record the oversight surface:

- what the human specified in advance;
- what approvals were required;
- what interventions occurred;
- whether interventions were routine or exceptional;
- what the human inspected before accepting the result;
- whether the system escalated appropriately;
- what would have happened without intervention.

This does not devalue human in the loop systems. It describes them honestly. In many settings, well designed oversight is the reason a system is trustworthy. The error is claiming the human contribution as machine autonomy.

## 9. Can, does repeatedly, and should be trusted to

Three propositions must remain separate:

1. **Can:** the system has produced the behavior at least once under authentic conditions.
2. **Does repeatedly:** the system produces the behavior with an observed rate and failure profile across relevant repetitions.
3. **Should be trusted to:** a decision maker is justified in relying on the system for a class of work, given consequences, oversight, authority, reversibility, and observed reliability.

The move from the first proposition to the second is empirical. The move from the second to the third is partly judgment. A 95 percent success rate may be excellent for drafting ideas and unacceptable for irreversible financial actions. The ladder can inform trust, but it cannot replace a consequence specific trust decision.

## 10. Memory, identity, and continuity

Upper tier work depends on continuity: knowing what was attempted, what was promised, what is still open, and what has already caused an effect. Memory in this sense is not a nostalgic transcript. It is operational state with provenance.

A useful memory system distinguishes at least:

- raw observations;
- decisions;
- commitments;
- task states;
- lessons and procedures;
- preferences and authority;
- unresolved questions.

Continuity can fail through omission, stale facts, false consolidation, or context leakage across principals. A system that remembers everything indiscriminately may be less trustworthy than one that remembers the right operational facts with clear scope and expiration.

## 11. Multi-agent truth

When several agents report on a task, agreement is not automatically independent confirmation. Agents may share a model, prompt, source, hidden assumption, or upstream artifact. Their reports can be correlated even when their wording differs.

The proper question is not "how many agents agree" but "how many independent evidential paths support the conclusion." Independent paths may involve different sources, direct destination checks, diverse methods, or a skeptic specifically tasked with finding a contradiction. Deliberation is valuable when it changes the evidence base or exposes a classification error. It is weak when it merely multiplies similar opinions.

## 12. The ladder as a research instrument

The ladder should be treated as a falsifiable instrument. It can be wrong in several productive ways:

- tiers may bundle properties that should be separated;
- a mandatory gate may be unnecessary for some domains;
- a missing property may matter more than an existing tier boundary;
- reliability thresholds may vary by consequence;
- cross system comparisons may break when environments differ too much;
- fleet behavior may require a branching model rather than a linear ladder.

Those possibilities belong in claims and deliberations. The aim is not reverence for nine labels. The aim is a progressively better account of what it means for an agent system to do increasingly difficult kinds of work and to deserve confidence while doing them.
